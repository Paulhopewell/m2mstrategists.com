Section 02

Where Your Business Is:
M2M Level Assessment

Chen Kitchen Solutions Ltd operates at M2M Level 2: Stability.

What This Level Means

Level 2 is the hardest level to escape, because it looks like success from the outside. Chen Kitchen Solutions Ltd has been trading for nine years. Revenue is £1.8M and growing. There are 22 people on the payroll, a recurring contract book worth £450,000, and a project manager who is, by all accounts, capable. The business is not struggling. The business is stable.

The problem with stable is what it hides. At Level 2, the owner is simultaneously the reason the business works and the reason it cannot grow beyond a certain point. Every system that should be documented is instead memorised. Every decision that should be delegated is instead reviewed. The business is predictable because David has made himself indispensable — and indispensability, in a business you intend to sell, is the single most destructive characteristic a founder can have.

Evidence from the Call

The following is not analysis. It is what David told Paul — and it places Chen Kitchen Solutions Ltd at Level 2 with unusual precision.

"Every quote over five thousand pounds needs my sign-off. Every complaint lands on my desk. I'm still doing the purchasing for major projects because I don't trust anyone else to get the margins right."

This is the Level 2 pattern in plain language: the owner as the operational hub, with no documented system standing between him and every significant decision.

"The way we quote, the way we purchase, the way we handle complaints — that's all in my head. Or in my inbox. Which is the same thing, really."

This is explicit confirmation that the core processes are person-dependent and undocumented. The inbox is not a system. It is a risk.

When Paul asked what happens if David is off sick for a week: "Quotes stop. Big decisions pile up... After about three days, things start to fray." — The business cannot function independently for 72 hours. Level 3 cannot begin until it can.

David also observed: "She creates leads, and then they land on my desk. I'm the bottleneck again." — Emma's marketing is generating Level 3 demand into a Level 2 infrastructure. The business cannot convert what it generates because the conversion process is David.

And perhaps most revealingly: "I spent the day on site sorting out a kitchen extraction issue that one of the engineers couldn't figure out. It was actually the most satisfying day of my week." — The Managing Director of a £1.8M business spent Wednesday with a screwdriver, and found it more satisfying than running the business. That gap — between what David does and what David should do — is the Level 2 ceiling made visible.

What's Working

Three things are genuinely strong and represent the foundations Level 3 is built on.

The maintenance contract book is the standout asset. Sixty clients, £450,000 recurring, 40% margin — this is a real, scalable revenue line that most Level 2 businesses do not have in this form. It also happens to be the part of the business most underserved by AI, meaning the upside is disproportionate.

David's self-awareness is unusual at this stage. Most Level 2 owners cannot name their constraint this clearly. David can. "I'm punishing the business for something that happened once." That sentence alone suggests the identity shift is accessible, not buried.

Emma's marketing function and the engineers' job management system are early infrastructure — small but real indicators that the transition to Level 3 has begun, even if it has not yet committed to it.

The Gap to Level 3 — Systemising

To reach Level 3, Chen Kitchen Solutions Ltd must move its quoting, purchasing, and complaint-handling processes out of David's head and into documented, delegatable systems. Darren must receive genuine operational authority over installations — not nominal authority with David as a silent backstop. The 15 hours per week David spends on quoting is not just wasted capacity; it is structural proof that Level 3 has not started.

The identity shift is equally critical. David's own admission — that screwdriver day was his most satisfying — confirms the gap is psychological as much as operational. AI can absorb the quoting burden and surface the maintenance data David instinctively values; it cannot make that identity shift for him. That is the work only David can do.

Section 03

Where Your Time and Money Is Leaking

Based on David's conversation with Paul, we've identified four areas where Chen Kitchen Solutions Ltd is losing time and money — often without David realising the full cost.

Drain 1: Deals Lost to Slow Manual Quoting £450,000/yr

David personally produces 8–10 quotes per week in Excel, each taking 30 minutes to 3 hours. End-to-end, from enquiry to delivery, the process can take a full week. Competitors quoting faster win the work before Chen Kitchen Solutions Ltd even submits.

"We lose work because we're slow, not because we're expensive."

David estimates two to three deals lost per month at an average job value of £15,000. That is £30,000–£45,000 in lost revenue every month — not on price, on speed. Secondary impact: the 15 hours per week David spends on quoting represents £51,750/year in MD time that could be redirected to growth.

Why it persists: A £30,000 extraction specification mistake three years ago convinced David that only he can be trusted with margin calculations. That single event has been setting the price for the business every month since.

Drain 2: MD Time Consumed by Technician-Level Work £41,400/yr

David — the Managing Director of a £1.8M business — regularly performs work his engineers or project manager should handle: on-site fixes, client visits for issues that could be diagnosed remotely, complaint management, and purchasing decisions.

"I'm doing thirty-pound-an-hour work when I should be doing three-hundred-pound-an-hour work."

Why it persists: David started as an engineer. Hands-on work feels like competence. The satisfaction he described on screwdriver day is real — and it is pulling him away from the strategic role the business needs from its MD.

Drain 3: Untapped Proactive Maintenance Revenue £200,000/yr

The engineers log equipment age, failure history, and condition data on every visit. Nobody analyses it. The maintenance model is entirely reactive. David knows the proactive revenue opportunity exists but has neither the time nor the system to surface it.

"If I could look at a client's kitchen and say — your walk-in freezer is eight years old, it's drawing more power than it should, it's going to fail within twelve months, here's what a replacement would cost and here's what you'd save on energy — that's a different conversation. That's proactive. That's valuable."

Why it persists: The data exists but is siloed in a job management app with no analytical layer above it. David is the only person who understands its commercial value — and his time is consumed entirely by Drains 1 and 2.

Drain 4: Team Capacity Suppressed by Owner-Dependency £33,000/yr

David's position as the single decision-making node creates a productivity tax across the entire team. The sales team operates at 70% capacity, waiting on approvals. Darren checks in on decisions he is capable of making alone. Emma generates demand that cannot be converted.

"I've trained them to be dependent on me."

Why it persists: The £5,000 quoting threshold, the retained purchasing authority, and the direct complaint-handling are all rational responses to that single extraction mistake three years ago. David named it himself: "I'm punishing the business for something that happened once."

The Pattern

Every drain traces back to the same root cause: David has built a Level 2 business that cannot function without him. This is not a competence deficit — David is self-aware, his team is capable, and the commercial foundations are solid. The constraint is structural. The quoting process, the purchasing authority, the maintenance data analysis, and the team's decision rights — all of them flow through David because no system exists to replace him. Until they do, every growth initiative hits the same wall.

AI-powered chatbot for your website that answers routine client queries automatically, routes complex queries to your team, and captures lead information.

£13.80/user/month

AI assistance built into Outlook, Word, Excel, and Teams. Drafts emails, summarises threads, extracts action items from meetings.

Pro plan ~£16–21/month

Workflow automation connecting Sage, your job management app, Outlook, and Excel — information flows between them without manual re-entry or David as the routing layer.

~£36/seat/month

Flexible database with AI Field Agents that analyse data, identify patterns, and generate summaries. Becomes your maintenance intelligence layer.

$0.99/resolution; ~£60–80/month steady state

AI agent that answers questions from your team and clients using a knowledge base you build. Fin only responds from documented content — it will not make things up.

If Chen Kitchen Solutions Ltd is taking the self-serve path, do these 10 things in order.

  1. Export your last 50 quotes from Excel — include job description, equipment specified, price, margin, and win/loss status. This is the training data for your quoting assistant. Without it, nothing else works. (Time: 2 hours)
  2. Build a Custom GPT using your quote data — sign up for ChatGPT Plus, open the GPT Builder, upload your quote exports, and write a simple instruction. Run 5 historical jobs through it to calibrate. (Time: 3–4 hours)

  4. Install Tidio on your website — create a free account, install the widget, write answers to your 10 most common enquiries. Add Lyro AI when you have enough chat history. (Time: 2–3 hours)
  5. Activate Microsoft 365 Copilot for David and Darren — toggle it on in your M365 admin panel. On day one, summarise your longest email thread and draft your next client update. (Time: 30 minutes)
  6. Record your baseline metrics — hours on quoting, open enquiries, pipeline value, response time to new leads. Store in a shared spreadsheet. (Time: 1–2 hours)
  7. Open a free HubSpot account and enter your 10 active deals — name, contact, value, stage, and next action. Ask one salesperson to update without David for two weeks. (Time: 1–2 hours setup; 15 mins/day)

Glossary

AI (Artificial Intelligence)
Software that performs tasks requiring human intelligence — understanding language, recognising patterns, making decisions. In this context, AI handles the routine and predictable parts of your workflow so your team can focus on the complex and relational.
Agent / AI Agent
An AI system that can take a sequence of actions to complete a task — not just answer a question, but look something up, compare options, and produce a recommendation.
Automation
A process that runs without human intervention once configured. When a new enquiry email arrives and Make automatically creates a task, that is automation.
API (Application Programming Interface)
The technical connection that allows two pieces of software to share data. Make uses APIs to connect Sage, your job management app, and Outlook without manual data re-entry.
Chatbot
An automated conversation interface. Tidio's Lyro AI handles routine client queries on your website; complex queries route to a human.
CRM (Customer Relationship Management)
Software that tracks every client and prospect interaction in one place. HubSpot is a CRM. A CRM is what makes a sales pipeline visible to a buyer in due diligence.
Custom GPT
A version of ChatGPT trained on documents and instructions you provide — configured to understand your specific pricing, equipment categories, and quoting logic.
EBITDA
Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation, and Amortisation. The profit measure most commonly used to value SMEs in a trade sale.
Knowledge Base
A structured collection of documented information — processes, procedures, FAQs — that the team can search without asking a person. This is what turns "all in David's head" into a transferable business asset.
M2M Framework
The five-level business and leader development model developed by Paul Hopewell (Paul Hopewell LLC). It maps the evolution of a business from survival to exit-ready.
Predictive Maintenance
Using data — equipment age, service history, energy patterns — to predict when equipment will fail, rather than waiting for failure. Transforms reactive repair into proactive asset management.
Workflow Automation
The automatic movement of work from one step to the next — enquiry to task, task to quote, completed job to invoice — without a human manually transferring information between systems.
